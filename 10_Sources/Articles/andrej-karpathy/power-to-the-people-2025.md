# Power to the People: How LLMs Flip the Script on Technology Diffusion

## Citation

Karpathy, Andrej. "Power to the People: How LLMs Flip the Script on Technology Diffusion". karpathy.bearblog.dev, 7 April 2025. https://karpathy.bearblog.dev/power-to-the-people/.

## One-line summary

Karpathy argues that LLMs reverse the historical top-down pattern of technology diffusion, delivering disproportionate gains to individual users rather than to governments and corporations.

## Key claims

- Historical technologies - electricity, cryptography, computing - diffused top-down from military and government to enterprise and eventually to individuals over decades.
- LLMs show the opposite pattern: individuals adopt them at scale first, while organisations lag due to legacy constraints, compliance requirements, and hallucination risk.
- ChatGPT became the fastest-growing consumer application in history, reaching 400 million weekly active users, confirming individual-first adoption.
- Organisations face structural barriers that individuals do not: security protocols, regulatory compliance, and institutional inertia all slow enterprise adoption.
- The democratisation advantage may reverse if performance scaling becomes purchasable only at high capital cost, concentrating superior AI capabilities in wealthy entities.
- The net current effect is a genuine redistribution of capability toward people who previously lacked access to expert knowledge.

## Excerpts

> "The future is already here, and it is shockingly distributed."
> ~ Conclusion

## Reveals about tendency of thought

- The technology-diffusion framing shows Karpathy positioning LLMs within economic history rather than treating them as sui generis, consistent with his Software 2.0 argument that LLMs are a new software paradigm rather than a new species.
- His warning about capital-driven reconcentration shows the limits of his optimism: he holds the democratisation thesis conditionally, not as inevitability.
- The 400 million user statistic anchors an abstract claim in concrete data, reflecting his recurring preference for empirical grounding over assertion.
